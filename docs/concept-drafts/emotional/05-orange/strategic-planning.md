# Emotional / Orange — Strategic-Planning Game Concept

> **Axis:** Planning the emotional life — structuring regulation across contexts and anticipating emotional demands.  **Why this axis for this module:** Orange treats emotion as skill. Skills can be planned for. The capacity to anticipate emotional challenges, pre-select strategies, and structure one's affective environment is the executive function of emotional intelligence.

---

## 1. Game Identity

**Name:** Affect Architect
**Fantasy:** The player designs emotional blueprints for upcoming challenges — mapping anticipated affective terrain, pre-positioning regulation resources, and building contingency plans for emotional surprises. They are the architect of their own emotional preparedness.

**Core loop:** Survey upcoming emotional terrain → anticipate affective demands → allocate regulation strategies → execute plan under live conditions → evaluate plan accuracy → refine.

## 2. Catalyst Delivery

**DA surfacing:** The Emotion Engineer over-plans. Every emotional contingency is mapped, every feeling pre-regulated. The plan becomes a cage — no room for spontaneous affect. Plans exclude the possibility of being surprised by feeling.
**DAll surfacing:** The Emotionally Illiterate cannot anticipate emotional demands. Plans are affect-blind — they prepare for logistical challenges but not emotional ones. "Why would I need a plan for feelings?"
**GA surfacing:** The Premature Empath plans for others' emotions but not their own. Blueprints are other-focused: "I'll hold space for their anxiety" without "I'll need support for my own."
**GAll surfacing:** The Regulation Fortress plans exclusively for self-containment. Every strategy is about maintaining walls. No plan includes receiving emotional support or being affected by others.

**Heal/Evolve path:** Plans must include "being with" (Agape) — spaces where regulation is deliberately not applied. Agency expressed through choosing when NOT to regulate.
**Evolve/Heal path:** Plans must include relational elements (Communion) — strategies that involve others. Eros expressed through planning for growth-edges.

## 3. Game Design

**Mechanic 1 — Terrain Survey:** Player receives a scenario description (job interview, difficult conversation, celebration, loss). Must identify 3–5 emotions likely to arise. Scored on accuracy and granularity.
**Mechanic 2 — Strategy Allocation:** Given anticipated emotions, player assigns regulation strategies from a toolkit. Each strategy has a "cost" (cognitive load) and "fit" (contextual appropriateness). Over-allocation is penalised as much as under-allocation.
**Mechanic 3 — Live Execution:** The scenario plays out (compressed time). Anticipated and unanticipated emotions arise. Player must adapt plan in real-time. Flexibility scored alongside preparedness.
**Mechanic 4 — Post-Action Review:** Player evaluates their plan's accuracy. "What did I anticipate correctly? What surprised me? What would I change?" Meta-cognitive reflection on emotional planning.

**Difficulty staircase:** Scenarios move from predictable (familiar contexts) to novel. Emotional complexity increases. Interpersonal elements introduced. Time pressure added.

## 5. Technical Requirements

- **Planning interface:** Drag-and-drop strategy allocation; visual timeline of anticipated emotional arc
- **Execution simulation:** Compressed-time scenario playback with decision points at emotional peaks
- **Scoring model:** Balance score (not too much, not too little planning), flexibility score (adaptation to surprises), accuracy score (prediction vs. reality)
- **Session length:** 6–10 minutes per full cycle (survey → allocate → execute → review)
- **Adaptive difficulty:** Scenario selection based on planning patterns — over-planners get chaotic scenarios; under-planners get complex ones
- **Shadow flags:** Over-allocation + no "being with" spaces → DA; affect-blind plans → DAll; other-focused plans + self-absent → GA; self-only plans + no relational strategies → GAll
